# L14. Rich or king: what founder control costs

## Goal
After this lab you can decide **which controls to keep and which to trade for money** as you raise: the board, the CEO job and a strong co-founder. You can size what each one costs and say which you chose, rich or king, and why.

## The principle
**The founder's dilemma** (Noam Wasserman, "The Founder's Dilemma", *Harvard Business Review*, Feb 2008; *The Founder's Dilemmas*, Princeton University Press, 2012): a founder who wants to build a very valuable company needs capital, experienced executives and board members. Getting them means giving up some control. A founder who keeps control of the board and the CEO job tends to build a less valuable company. So the founder must choose a goal, and the goal changes the right moves:

- **Rich:** build the most valuable company possible, and accept stepping back from the CEO job or the board to get there.
- **King:** stay in charge, and accept a smaller company or a smaller euro outcome.

Wasserman's point is that neither is wrong. The costly mistake is to want both without noticing the trade-off, and to find out only when a term sheet forces the question.

**Why it happens:** early on, value comes from the founder's vision and skills. After the first years it depends on what the company can attract: money, people who have scaled before, advisers and board members. Those resources arrive on conditions, and the conditions are mostly control. Investors also tend to replace the CEO when the company outgrows the founder.

## The ideal and the trade-offs
**There is no universal ideal; there is a deliberate choice.** The ideal is to decide, before the first term sheet, which one or two controls you cannot live without and to buy exactly those, not all of them.

| Goal | Do this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Rich** (most euros) | Trade control for resources early: investor-led board, an experienced CEO when the company outgrows you, a strong co-founder with equity | Each control a founder keeps goes with a 17-22% lower valuation (Wasserman 2017); equity given for a CEO or co-founder is much cheaper |
| **King** (stay in charge) | Keep the CEO job and enough of the board to protect it; accept a smaller outcome | The CEO job lives at the board's pleasure; a balanced board with you in the chair is the cheap middle |
| **Both, as far as possible** | Keep the **one** control you could not live without (for example the chair); trade the rest | In the lab's model this keeps about 80% of the richest route's euros and still gives you a say |

**Rules of thumb**
- Each control you hold (CEO job, board chair, board majority) costs about **20% of company value**; three controls cost about half.
- Giving equity for a CEO or co-founder is usually cheaper than keeping the control they would replace, but only if they really lift the company's value.
- Do not buy control that does not protect what you want: a board majority with a hired CEO pays the price of control but does not keep you in the CEO chair.

| Lever | Effect (model) | Cost and catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Give investors the board** | Large: about 1.5× your euros vs keeping the keys | You can be replaced as CEO; assumed odds of still being CEO fall from about 85% to about 30% |
| **Balanced board, you chair** | Medium: about 1.2× | Needs a lead investor who accepts it; the cheapest way to stay CEO with better-than-even odds |
| **Hire an experienced CEO** | Large, from year 3 onwards | Costs about 7% of the equity (assumption) and your CEO title |
| **Add a strong co-founder** | About +10% euros if the company is worth 25% more (assumption) | Takes about 12% of the equity; only pays if the value lift is real |
| **Keep every control** | Worst for euros: about half the value, roughly 0.56× the pure-money route | The only route that really keeps you king |

## Tests (in the simulation; answers for the teacher)
Setting: you are founder-CEO in mid-2027, own 60% after the seed, and two rounds follow. A good exit with no founder control is worth about €400m; each control kept cuts value by 19.5%; each round dilutes 20%. Today (all controls): value €209m, your stake 38%, your euros €80m.
1. **Be rich.** Keep the keys €80m; share the board and stay CEO €99m (1.2×); give the board, stay CEO €124m (1.5×); **open everything (investor board, hired CEO, strong co-founder) €157m (2.0×)**. Best: open everything. The instinct (keep control) is the worst option.
2. **Be king.** Stay CEO, buy the cheapest board deal that gives better-than-even odds of running it. Majority €80m, 85% odds; **balanced board you chair €99m, 55%**; investor-led €124m, 30%; hired CEO with a board majority €93m (no CEO job). Best: balanced board. Trap: the majority without the CEO chair.
3. **Middle path.** Step back as CEO but keep at least one control. Investors lead €143m (no control); **balanced board, you chair, plus a strong co-founder €126m**; chair alone €115m; investors lead plus co-founder €157m (no control). Best: chair plus co-founder, 80% of the richest route.

## Evidence
- **Valuation and control, large sample.** Wasserman, "The throne vs. the kingdom: Founder control and value creation in startups", *Strategic Management Journal* 38(2), 2017, pp. 255-277 (DOI 10.1002/smj.2478): in a dataset of 6,130 US startups, each additional level of founder control (such as holding the CEO job and board control) is associated with a 17.1-22.0% lower pre-money valuation, especially for startups three years old or more. A write-up in *strategy+business* says the startups were high-potential US companies launched 2005-2012 and also reports a 35.8-51.4% lower amount of financing raised per level. *(Read from the abstract as quoted by a search summary and from that write-up; the paper itself is not read in full. A blog quotes a different range, 23.0-58.1%, which I could not trace: not yet verified.)*
- **Founders replaced as CEO.** Wasserman, "The Founder's Dilemma", *HBR*, Feb 2008: 212 US start-ups from the late 1990s and early 2000s. A secondary article (Utah Business) reports about half of founders no longer CEO in year three, about 40% still CEO in year four, and fewer than 25% leading the IPO. Another secondary page (entrepreneurship.org) says almost 50% of founding CEOs are replaced within four years, about 75% of them fired. *(Figures come from secondary sources; the claim "replaced by the third round" is not found in anything I read: not yet verified.)*
- **Why replacement rises with funding.** Harvard Business School *Working Knowledge*, "The Founding CEO's Dilemma: Stay or Go?": the share of founder-CEOs who go the distance is very low in high-potential ventures, and replacement odds rise sharply when a new financing round is raised. Qualitative; no figures given.
- **The investor view.** The same page describes a "Rich versus King" screen used by an early-stage fund: it avoids backing founders who want to be king.
- **The book.** *The Founder's Dilemmas* (Princeton University Press, 2012) builds on a large founder survey (about 10,000 founders, per a review: not yet verified).

## Where it breaks
- **Correlation, not proof of cause.** Better companies may get investors who demand fewer controls, and weaker ones may be forced to give them up; the study cannot fully separate these. Check how Wasserman handles this in the paper before leaning on it (not yet verified).
- **Founder-dependent businesses.** Where the founder is the scarce technical asset (deep tech) the "after year 3" logic may arrive later, and replacing the founder can destroy value.
- **Terms differ from titles.** A balanced board with a founder chair can be strong or weak depending on vetoes, drag-along and good-leaver terms (not yet verified here; ask a lawyer).
- **Leverage changes the price.** Competing term sheets and non-dilutive grants can let a founder keep more control at a smaller price than the average suggests.
- **The model is a stylisation.** The 20% per control is multiplicative here; the odds of staying CEO, the 7% for a hired CEO and the co-founder's 25% lift are assumptions, not findings.

## Application question (graded on rubric B)
> It is late 2027. Your new company has a first prototype, three pilot customers and a €1.5m seed behind it; you own about 60%. A strong deep-tech fund offers to lead your Series A, but wants a board with two investor seats, one independent and you, and a right to review the CEO after 18 months. Another option is a slower round built on grants and customer prepayments, where you keep the board.
> 1. Which controls do you hold now, and roughly what does each one cost you in company value if Wasserman's finding applies? No calculation: use the rule of thumb.
> 2. Are you playing rich or king? State your choice, then say which controls you keep and which you give, and what you would ask for in the term sheet.
> 3. Where could this evidence mislead you in this case?

Answer in under 300 words.

## Takeaways
1. **Control has a price:** in Wasserman's data on 6,130 startups, each extra level of founder control (such as the CEO job or the board) goes with a 17-22% lower pre-money valuation. Keeping all three can roughly halve what the company is worth.
2. **Rich or king is a choice to make before the term sheet.** Trading control for resources early left more euros in the model (about 2×), even on a smaller stake.
3. **A king buys the cheapest control that works:** the CEO job without the board behind you is a coin flip, and a board majority without the CEO chair pays for control but does not buy the job.
4. **The middle path:** keep the one control you need (for example the chair), hand over the rest, and add a strong co-founder only if he or she lifts value by more than the 12% equity costs.
