# L16. ZOPA, BATNA and the first number

## Goal
After this lab you can decide **who names a number first and how high**, which preparation lever to pull before a negotiation (your alternative, your argument or your opening price), and **what to trade** when the other side pushes on price.

## The principle
Three ideas that fit together:

- **BATNA** (best alternative to a negotiated agreement; Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes*, 1981): what you will do if the talks fail. It is the source of your walk-away power. Your **reservation price** is the point where the deal is exactly as good as your BATNA.
- **ZOPA** (zone of possible agreement; the idea of a bargaining zone between reservation prices is usually credited to Howard Raiffa, *The Art and Science of Negotiation*, 1982): a deal is possible only between the seller's lowest acceptable price and the buyer's highest. Each side sees only its own edge of the zone.
- **Anchoring** (Galinsky & Mussweiler, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2001): the first number on the table pulls the final price toward itself, for whichever side says it.

On top of this: **value can be created, not just divided.** When the two sides care about different issues, trading across issues (logrolling) makes the zone itself bigger.

| Idea | Question it answers | Your move |
|---|---|---|
| BATNA | How strong am I if I walk? | Improve it before the meeting |
| ZOPA | Is there a deal at all, and how big is the room? | Estimate their limit, know yours |
| Anchor | Where will the price land inside the zone? | Name the first number, ambitious but defensible |
| Logrolling | Can I make the zone bigger? | Trade what is cheap for you against what is dear |

## The ideal and the trade-offs
**What to aim for:** a deal inside the zone, as close to the other side's limit as they will still sign, reached with terms that leave both sides better off than the price alone would.

**Rules of thumb**
1. **Know your walk-away number and your alternative before you speak.** A negotiator without a credible alternative negotiates against themselves.
2. **Open first when you have prepared**, at or a little above what you think they could pay, with reasons ready. Waiting lets their number pull you.
3. **Prepare the alternative, then the argument, then the opening price.** In that order of value.
4. **Never concede on one issue alone.** Ask for something on another issue in return.

| Lever | Effect in the lab model | Cost and catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Improve your BATNA** (a second customer in talks) | Biggest preparation lever: about +€35 a unit (+4%) in Test 2 | Takes weeks and founder time; the alternative must be real |
| **Name the first number, ambitiously** | About 2.6× the extra money of waiting (Test 1) | Too high (here about 10% above their limit) raises the risk of a walkout |
| **Build a stronger value case** | Smaller: about +€15 a unit | Cheap, and it lifts what they will pay; effect depends on their belief |
| **Trade across issues** | About 2.8× holding firm in Test 3 (larger zone, bigger volume) | Needs issues the two sides value differently; big customers may have fixed policies |
| **Open low to look reasonable** | Negative: about −€35 a unit | You give them a lower anchor for nothing |
| **Split the difference** | Small: about 1.1× holding firm | Fair-feeling but leaves value on the table |

**Founder rule:** your first paid pilot with a large customer sets your reference price and your story to investors. A day of preparing the alternative and the number is worth more than a week of polishing slides.

## Tests (in the simulation; answers for the teacher)
Setting: a first paid pilot of 200 hardware units. Viktor's fallback is a smaller buyer at about €600 a unit; the customer would pay up to about €900; procurement opens near €560. Cash is dear for a startup (about 36% a year) and cheap for a large customer (about 6%) in the model. The model is illustrative: price moves 37 cents per euro of opening number; the customer walks if it keeps under a quarter of the gain (assumption).
1. **Who names a number first?** Wait for them (about €15k above the fallback on 200 units) vs open at €750 (€28k, 1.9×) vs **open at €900 (€39k, 2.6×)** vs open at €1,100 (€14.5k, 0.96×; chance of a deal drops to 26%). Best: **C**. The instinct (let them go first) is the weak answer, and so is the very high opening.
2. **Best preparation lever** (opening at €900): **second customer in talks at about €700 (+€35 a unit, to about €830)** vs a stronger value case (+€15) vs opening lower to look reasonable (−€35). Best: **A**.
3. **They insist on €60 off** (opened at €1,000, price about €842): hold firm (€35k, deal chance 73%) vs split the difference (€40k, 1.1×) vs **trade the €60 for payment in 15 days and 400 units (€97k, 2.8×; zone grows from €300 to about €358 a unit)** vs give the €60 for nothing (€35k, 1.0×). Best: **C**.

## Evidence
- **First offers anchor the outcome.** Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001), *JPSP* 81(4), 657-669: three experiments with MBA students negotiating the price of a simulated sale of a pharmaceutical plant, face to face and by email. Whoever made the first offer, buyer or seller, got the better price; in the control groups the correlation between first offer and final price was .80 to .93. When the receiving side focused on the other side's alternatives or reservation price, or on its own target, the advantage disappeared (correlations of about .30 to .40). *(Setting, sample and correlations are taken from the abstract and a secondary summary, not from the paper itself: not yet verified.)*
- **Size across many studies.** A meta-analysis of simulated negotiations by Orr & Guthrie (*Ohio State Journal on Dispute Resolution*, vol. 21, 2005/2006) is summarised by Harvard's Program on Negotiation as: each extra euro in the anchor raised the final agreement by about 37 cents. Another page quotes a pooled correlation of about .50 (49.7 cents); the sources differ, so the lab model uses the lower figure. *(Not checked against the paper.)* The same summary says even very unreasonable anchors have a large effect.
- **Strong alternatives lead people to open first.** Magee, Galinsky & Gruenfeld (2007), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 33, 200-212: negotiators with strong alternatives were more than three times as likely to say they would make the first offer, and moving first gave an advantage. *(From a university abstract; details not verified.)*
- **Weak alternatives complicate the rule.** Maaravi, Heller & Levy (2023, *Negotiation Journal*), as reported by Harvard PON: in a job-offer simulation, 13% of low-power candidates wanted to open against 31% of high-power ones, and low-power people often asked for less than the other side would have offered. With a weak BATNA, waiting can be the better move.
- **Value creation.** Thompson & Hastie (1990), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 47, 98-123: most negotiators expect the other side's interests to be fully opposed to theirs, and a substantial number fail to notice interests that are fully compatible, settling for worse deals than were available. Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner (1999-2003) found that students who compared two cases were nearly three times as likely to use a value-creating strategy in a later negotiation. *(Both read as abstracts or summaries only.)*
- **Concepts.** The reservation point as the indifference point between a deal and your BATNA, and the ZOPA as the overlap, are described in Harvard PON material; the attribution of ZOPA to Raiffa (1982) and of BATNA to Fisher & Ury (1981) comes from a secondary source and has not been checked against the books *(not yet verified)*.

## Where it breaks
- **You never see their reservation price.** The zone is an estimate. If you misjudge it, an ambitious opening ends in no deal. Test their limit with questions before you name a number.
- **Anchors that are not credible** cost trust, and a large customer is a repeat player and a reference. Prepare reasons for the number.
- **Weak BATNA.** If you have no alternative and need the deal, the first-offer advantage is less clear (Maaravi et al.). Fix the alternative first.
- **Prepared counterparts.** A procurement team that studies your alternatives and its own target is largely immune to anchors (Galinsky & Mussweiler). Do not expect the opening to work on a skilled buyer without reasons.
- **Logrolling needs different priorities.** If the customer's payment terms are fixed by company policy, that trade does not exist. Check what is really negotiable.
- **Be truthful about the alternative.** A claimed second customer that does not exist is a lie that can be tested and damages trust (and may raise legal issues; this is not legal advice).
- **The lab is one price and three issues, at one moment.** Real negotiations are repeated, multi-party and emotional.

## Application question (graded on rubric B)
> It is 2027. Your new company is in talks for its first paid pilot with a large industrial customer. Their procurement team has sent their standard contract and a price well below what you hoped for. You have no second customer yet.
> 1. What is your BATNA today, and what would you do in the next six weeks to improve it? Size roughly what it is worth.
> 2. Do you name the first number? What number, and how do you choose it?
> 3. If they push on price, what do you trade, and what do you ask for in return?
> 4. Where could this framework mislead you in this negotiation?

Answer in under 300 words.

## Takeaways
1. Walk-away power comes from your BATNA, and a deal is possible only inside the ZOPA between both sides' reservation prices. You see only your own edge.
2. The first number anchors the outcome. Open first, at or a little above what you think they would pay, with reasons. An absurd anchor raises the risk of a walkout.
3. Improve your BATNA before you polish your argument: a second customer moves your floor and is usually the biggest preparation lever.
4. Trade across issues instead of splitting the price: concede what is cheap for you and dear to them, and never concede on one issue without asking for something on another.
