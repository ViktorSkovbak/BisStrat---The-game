# L15. Managerial leverage: spend your hours where they multiply others

## Goal
After this lab you can decide **where a founder-CEO's hours go** (own work, hiring, priorities and specs, 1:1s and unblocking, investors and customers) and how that split must change when the team grows or your time shrinks.

## The principle
**Managerial leverage** (Andrew Grove, *High Output Management*, 1983; read via summaries, book not read): a manager's output is the output of the organisation they lead, plus that of neighbouring organisations they influence. It is not how much work the manager personally produces. Leverage is the output generated by one unit of a given activity, so the job is to find the few activities that move many people's output and spend time there.

> **manager's output = the sum over activities of (leverage of the activity × amount of the activity)**

Summaries of the book give three sources of high leverage: an action that **reaches many people**, an action that is short but **changes someone's work for a long time** (teaching, setting a priority), and a piece of **key information** that shapes the work of a group. Timing matters: the same action is worth more early.

**Why it happens:** one person's hour can change what ten people do for a week. The same hour spent on your own task changes one person's output. But each activity saturates: once the team has clear priorities and its blockers are gone, the next hour there adds little.

## The ideal and the trade-offs
**The goal is to maximise the output of the whole company, not your own busyness and not to vanish from the work.** Spend the next hour where it adds most, and stop when another activity adds more. Your own work has a place while the team is small and you are the best engineer or seller; its share shrinks as the team grows.

Rules of thumb (illustrative, from the lab's model; not measured values):

| Situation | Where the hours go |
|---|---|
| 12 people, ~55 h | About 5-10 h own work, ~6 h hiring, ~13 h priorities and specs, ~12 h 1:1s and unblocking, ~17 h investors and customers |
| 30 people, same 55 h | Own work near 0; more into priorities, 1:1s and a few trained leads; hiring only as fast as you can onboard |
| Only 40 h | Cut your own work first; protect direction, unblocking and external time |

| Lever | What it multiplies | Cost and catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Priorities and clear specs** | Everyone's effort points at the right work | Needs decisions you may not want to make; vague priorities add little |
| **1:1s, unblocking, coaching** | Removes stalls; raises each person's skill and maturity (Grove: raise it, then loosen your style) | Hours needed grow with headcount; about 6-8 direct reports is a practical limit (via a summary) |
| **Hiring** | More hands, but slow: months to find, months to ramp | Takes your time twice (hiring and onboarding); returns arrive mostly in year two |
| **Investors and customers** | Keeps cash and demand coming; without it, output meets no buyer | Saturates: past what the stage needs, more meetings add little |
| **Your own engineering or sales work** | One pair of hands (a founder's pair may be worth several) | Everything else waits while you do it; fine early, wrong at scale |

## Tests (in the simulation; answers for the teacher)
Setting: the page's illustrative model; the founder's week split over five activities. "Adds" = output your week adds to the team compared with the same team left alone (the ratio is the "vs today" column).
1. **Ten more hours** (today 45 h: 20 own work, 3 hiring, 4 priorities, 5 1:1s, 13 external; 12 people). +10 own work (adds 1.11×; company output +5.9%) vs +10 hiring (1.08×; +4.2%) vs +5 priorities and +5 1:1s (**1.18×; +10.3%**) vs +10 external (1.09×; +5.1%). Best: C. The instinct (own work) is second: it adds one pair of hands.
2. **The team grows to 30**, with the 12-person split unchanged: per-person output falls from 86% to 78% of fully supported (−9%). Keep the split (1.00×), **own work into priorities and 1:1s (1.08×; company +4.4%; per person back to 85%)**, own work into hiring (0.94×; −3%), own work into external time (1.00×). Best: B.
3. **Only 40 hours** (cut 15 from 15/6/9/10/15). Stop own work (0.87×; company −8%), drop 1:1s and trim own work (0.78×; −13%), drop investors and customers (0.55×; −27%), cut everything by the same share (0.82×; −11%). Best: A.

## Evidence
- **Does management matter in practice?** Bloom, Eifert, Mahajan, McKenzie & Roberts (QJE 2013, 128(1), 1-51): a field experiment on large Indian textile firms. Randomly chosen plants received free management consulting; others were controls. Adopting the practices raised **productivity by about 17% in the first year**, through better quality and efficiency, and cut inventory; within three years the treated firms opened more plants. The authors point to missing information, not cost, as the main reason the practices had not been adopted, and to limits on managers' time as a brake on reallocation across firms. *(Sample size and run length: not yet verified; only the abstract was read.)*
- **Do management practices vary, and do they track results?** Bloom & Van Reenen (QJE 2007, 122(4), 1351-1408): a survey of **732 medium-sized manufacturing firms** in the US, France, Germany and the UK. Better measured management goes with higher productivity, profitability, sales growth and survival. Quality varies widely within every country, with a long tail of badly managed firms; weak competition and family firms that pass control to the eldest son together explain about half of that tail. *(Abstract level; the scoring method is not yet verified. A correlation, not proof that management causes the results; the 2013 experiment supplies the causal evidence.)*
- **Grove's leverage idea itself.** Read via summaries (Substack and Helcim pages, a16z), not the book: the output definition, the three sources of leverage, training and motivation as high-leverage work, a half-day a week per direct report, and task-relevant maturity. *(Page numbers and exact wording: not yet verified. Specific "hours of teaching equal months of output" examples appear only on unofficial mirror copies and are not used.)*
- **What the lab's numbers are:** illustrative. The model is tuned so that moving from thin to decent direction and 1:1 time lifts output per person by about 12%, the same order as the 17% above. The 2.5× weight on the founder's own hour, the 35 hours per hire and the 7-hour customer-pull saturation are teaching assumptions *(not yet verified against any data)*.

## Where it breaks
- **Leverage can be negative:** unprepared meetings, vague priorities or meddling lower others' output instead of raising it. More manager hours are not automatically better.
- **Information is half the job:** the 1:1 is how you learn where the blockers are. Replacing it with a dashboard loses the cheap signal.
- **Spans and layers:** past about 8 direct reports, 1:1s stop scaling; the lever becomes training leads, and a bad lead has leverage too.
- **Early on your own work is the output:** with 3 people and the founder as the best engineer, the multiplier is small. The law bites as headcount rises.
- **Delegation without information** does not create leverage; it creates drift.
- **The model ignores fatigue.** A founder at 70 hours a week makes worse calls; nothing here charges for it.

## Application question (graded on rubric B)
> It is mid-2027. Your hardware company has 12 people and plans to reach 25 within a year. You work 55 hours: about 25 on your own engineering and sales work, 5 on hiring, 5 on priorities and specs, 5 on 1:1s, 15 with investors, grant officers and pilot customers. A seed investor asks how you will keep output per person high as you double.
> 1. State the law in your own words and say what it predicts for how your 55 hours should look at 25 people.
> 2. Size it roughly: which change matters most, and about how much does it add?
> 3. Give the concrete policy: what you stop, start and protect, and the trigger for each.
> 4. Where could the law mislead you in a deep-tech company?

Answer in under 300 words. No calculation needed.

## Takeaways
1. A manager's output is the team's output (Grove). Judge an hour by what it adds to everyone else's work.
2. Spend the next hour where it changes the most people's output: priorities, specs, 1:1s, unblocking, hiring and training. Returns diminish, so move on once a lever is covered.
3. The same split covers less as the team grows: at 30 people a 12-person split lowers output per person by about 9% (model). Shift your own work into direction and 1:1s and build trained leads.
4. When hours are short, cut your own work first, never what multiplies others or brings demand in. Cutting every activity equally is the common mistake.
