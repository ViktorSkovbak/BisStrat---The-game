# L09. Price is the strongest profit lever

## Goal
After this lab you can decide **where profit comes from**: which lever moves it most, what a discount really costs in extra volume, and how to set a price from what the customer gains instead of from your cost or a rival's price.

## The principle
**Profit = (price − variable cost) × units − fixed costs.** A euro of extra price goes straight to profit. A euro of lower cost does too, but only on the units you sell. An extra unit brings only its **margin**, not its price.

**McKinsey's price study** (Marn & Rosiello, *Harvard Business Review*, Sept-Oct 1992): for the average company in a study of about 2,400 firms, a **1% price improvement with volume unchanged** lifted operating profit about **11%**. The same 1% on variable cost gave about 8%, on volume about 3%, on fixed cost about 2%.

| 1% improvement in | Operating profit gain (average company) |
|---|---|
| Price | about 11% |
| Variable cost | about 8% |
| Volume | about 3% |
| Fixed cost | about 2% |

**The catch:** that 11% assumes no customer walks away. A price rise pays as long as the units you lose are fewer than **rise ÷ (margin + rise)**. The other side of the same arithmetic is the **discount trap**: a discount needs extra volume of **discount ÷ (margin − discount)** just to earn what you earned before.

| Margin | 5% off | 10% off | 15% off | 20% off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20% | 33% more units | 100% | 300% | never (no margin left) |
| 40% | 14% | 33% | 60% | 100% |
| 60% | 9% | 20% | 33% | 50% |

## The ideal and the trade-offs
**The goal is not the highest price. It is the price that leaves the customer a clear gain and you the most profit.** The right price sits between your **cost (the floor)** and the **customer's economic value (the ceiling)**: what their best alternative costs plus what your product adds for them (Nagle & Holden's framing of economic value).

| Rule of thumb | Why |
|---|---|
| Start from the customer's gain, not your cost | Cost-plus ignores what the product is worth; it can leave a lot of money on the table or, with thin margins, price too low to cover fixed costs |
| Do not match a rival's price for a weaker product | You copy their margin and their cost base, not your value |
| Never take nearly all the customer's gain | Adoption collapses once the customer's payback gets thin |
| Compute the break-even volume before any discount | 15% off at a 40% margin needs 60% more units |
| Give something specific for something specific | A longer contract, a volume commitment, a reference: each can be costed |

| Lever | Effect | Cost and catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Raise price** | Biggest per percent | Volume loss; needs proof of value and a clear reason to the customer |
| **Cut variable cost** | Large, no volume risk | Engineering time; quality risk; slow |
| **Win more volume** | Medium, and only at the margin | Needs sales effort; discounts eat the gain |
| **Cut fixed cost** | Smallest per percent | Can cut the capacity you need to grow |

**Founder rule:** your first prices set your reference price with customers and investors. It is far easier to start a little high and give a specific concession than to raise a price you have already discounted.

## Tests (in the simulation; answers for the teacher)
Setting: price €2,000, variable cost €1,200 (40% margin), about 1,000 units, fixed costs €700k, profit €100k. A typical customer saves about €5,000 in year one. Model: the share of prospects who buy is about 92% at €2,000, falls slowly, and half walk at €3,000 (stylised, not measured).
1. **Which 5% matters most?** +5% volume €140k (1.4×), **+5% price €176k (1.8×)**, −5% unit cost €160k (1.6×), −5% fixed cost €135k (1.35×). Best: price. The instinct (volume) comes third. With no volume loss the price rise would give €200k (2×).
2. **The big customer wants 15% off.** Accept (+400 units, €1,700): about €27k (0.3×), below break-even; counter 8% off (+300 units): about €153k (1.5×); say no: €100k; **hold the price, add a €60-a-unit service plan for +250 units: about €225k (2.25×)**. Break-even for 15% off is 1,600 units; the customer offers about 1,450.
3. **How to set the price.** Cost-plus €2,185: about €232k (2.3×); match the rival €1,800: about **−€76k**; **half the customer's saving, €2,500: about €359k (3.6×)**, 75% of prospects buy; 80% of the saving, €4,000: about −€240k, 15% of prospects buy. In the model the peak is about €2,600.

## Evidence
- **McKinsey study.** Marn & Rosiello, *Harvard Business Review*, Sept-Oct 1992: the price, variable cost, volume and fixed cost effects above, for an average company. Figures come from secondary write-ups that cite the exhibit (about 2,400 companies; some sources give 2,463, from the Compustat database); the HBR text itself was not opened, so the exact company count is *(not yet verified)*. Later write-ups round the 11.1% to about 11%.
- **Value-based pricing.** Nagle & Holden, *The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing*: price from economic value to the customer, meaning the price of the best alternative (reference value) plus the value of what makes your offer different (differentiation value). Taken from catalogue and review summaries of the book; the book's own wording and page numbers are *(not yet verified)*.
- **A damaging price-policy change.** J.C. Penney, fiscal 2012 (JCK, 28 Feb 2013): after dropping most coupons and sale events for "fair and square" everyday prices, total sales fell 24.8% to $12.985 billion and the company lost $985 million; the CEO called the pricing policy a mistake and sales and coupons were brought back. That sales fell *because of* the price policy alone is *(not yet verified)*: other changes were made at the same time.
- **Industrial discount habits.** The 40%-margin and 33%-volume discount figures here are arithmetic, not data. A real B2B case of discount leakage was *(not yet verified)* and is left out.

## Where it breaks
- **Volume loss.** The 11% figure assumes customers stay. In price-sensitive or commodity markets a 1% rise can lose more than 1% of volume.
- **Customers learn.** A discount that others hear about becomes the new reference price (the J.C. Penney case also shows that customers used to discounts react badly when they vanish).
- **Strategic pricing.** Low early prices can be rational to win references, build a learning curve or lock in distribution. The law counts profit this year, not what the early customer is worth later.
- **Value is hard to measure.** The customer's saving is an estimate; real buyers need proof (a pilot) and a payback they trust.
- **The demand curve in this lab is stylised.** The shape is invented; the lesson is the arithmetic, not the optimum price.

## Application question (graded on rubric B)
> It is mid-2027. Your company sells a physical product to industrial customers at about a 40% margin. Your first large pilot customer says it will order 20 units, and a follow-on of perhaps 100, if you give it 20% off list price.
> 1. Size it roughly: how much more volume would you need, just to break even on the discount? (Use the table above; no calculation needed.)
> 2. What do you answer, and what could you offer instead of a price cut?
> 3. Where could this law mislead you in a young company?

Answer in under 300 words.

## Takeaways
1. Price is the strongest profit lever: in McKinsey's study of about 2,400 companies, 1% on price (volume unchanged) lifted operating profit about 11%, against about 8% for variable cost, 3% for volume and 2% for fixed cost.
2. A discount needs units = discount ÷ (margin − discount): with a 40% margin, 10% off needs about 33% more units and 15% off needs 60%.
3. Trade something specific for something specific (commitment, term, costed service), not price for hoped-for volume.
4. Price from the customer's value, not your cost or a rival's price: the best price sits between your cost and the customer's gain, and you find it by testing.
