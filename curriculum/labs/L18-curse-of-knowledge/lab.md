# L18. The curse of knowledge: why your strategy lands less than you think

## Goal
After this lab you can decide **how to get a strategy into a team's daily decisions**: which format to use, how often to repeat it, how to tie it to real choices, and how to check that it landed without trusting your own feeling of clarity.

## The principle
**The curse of knowledge** (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, *Journal of Political Economy*, 1989): once you know something, you cannot easily set that knowledge aside and judge how things look to someone who does not know it. The paper challenged the standard assumption in economic models that a better-informed party can reproduce the judgement of a less-informed one.

**The tapping study** (Elizabeth Newton, Stanford PhD, 1990, as retold in Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick*, 2007): one person taps the rhythm of a well-known song on a table; a listener tries to name it. Tappers expected listeners to succeed about half the time. Listeners succeeded in about 3 of 120 tries, roughly 1 in 40. *(Numbers via secondary sources only; Newton's dissertation not read.)*

**Why it happens:** the tapper hears the tune in their head while tapping; the listener hears knocks. A leader hears the whole strategy (markets, pilots, trade-offs, history) behind every sentence; the team hears the sentence. You judge clarity by what you understand, and you understand everything.

**Why repetition matters:** memory decays fast after one exposure and slowly afterwards (Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve, 1880s; replicated by Murre & Dros, 2015). Each repeat, especially a spaced one, makes the memory last longer.

## The ideal and the trade-offs
**The goal is not a perfect document. It is a strategy that appears in the team's decisions.** A strategy is only useful at the moment someone decides which request to take, what to demo, what to refuse.

| Aim for | Why |
|---|---|
| **One sentence** a new hire could repeat | Short enough to survive forgetting and retelling |
| **A real example every time** | Concrete cases stick and show how to apply it |
| **Many tellings in many places** | Each repeat extends memory; each channel reaches someone else |
| **Live decisions** that use it | Retrieval and use beat re-reading; they also show you who is lost |

**Rules of thumb:** say it more than feels necessary (Kotter's line is 10 times or more); never judge clarity by your own sense or by a silent room; check the group furthest from your background first.

| Lever | Effect in the lab | Cost and catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Cut the message to one sentence + real examples** | Large (the memo alone: 1.7×; the sentence adds concreteness) | Costs you the hard thinking of choosing what matters; the detail must live elsewhere |
| **Repeat, weekly, in 2+ channels** | Very large: even the full deck repeated weekly reaches about 31% vs 2% | Feels repetitive to you; it is not to them |
| **Tie to real decisions** (weekly live cases) | Largest in the model (about 7× on a sentence launched once) | Takes 15-20 minutes a week of leader time; exposes where people are lost, which is the point |
| **Send the long document** | Almost nothing (about 1.1×) | Feels thorough; mostly unread and unretained; useful only as reference |
| **Ask "any questions?"** | Nothing: silence is not understanding | Cheap, flattering, and misleading |

**Founder rule:** with 15-20 people, the strategy is not a deck but a sentence you can test. If a new engineer cannot use it to decide a real request in under a minute, you have not communicated it yet.

## Tests (in the simulation; answers for the teacher)
Setting: 15 engineers and 3 sales people; this year's strategy: win and keep three paid pilot customers and build only what helps that. Measure: share who can state and apply it after 4 weeks.
1. **The launch.** Deck once (2.4%, today) vs memo once (4.2%, 1.7×) vs **one sentence weekly with an example in two channels (59%, 24×)** vs deck twice in two channels (13%, 5.4×). Best: the sentence. The instinct (the thorough deck) is the worst. Repetition alone is worth a lot: deck weekly in two channels reaches 31%.
2. **Whom you overestimate.** You guess about 74% have it. Truth after a month with the deck: 3.3% among the engineers who helped build it (gap 22×), 2.4% typical (30×), 0.9% for sales (**85×, 2.8× worse than typical**). Best: sales. Even the closest colleagues do not hold it after one telling.
3. **The next step.** After the sentence is said once (10%): repeat weekly 59% (6.0×) vs **tie it to real cases 70% (7.1×)** vs send the long document 10.6% (1.1×) vs nothing (1.0×). Best: real cases, with repeating a close second; the long document does nothing.

## Evidence
- **The original finding.** Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (*J. Political Economy* 97(5), 1989, 1232-1254): in experiments, better-informed people could not ignore what they knew when predicting what less-informed people would do. The abstract says that more information is not always better for the informed party. *(Only the abstract was read; the statement that market forces cut the effect by about half comes from a search summary of the full paper: not yet verified.)*
- **Tapping.** Newton (1990) via Heath & Heath (2007): tappers predicted about 50% success; listeners got about 2.5% (3 of 120). Secondary accounts differ on details: one lists 25 songs for tappers to choose from; one gives the year as 1991. *(Not yet verified against the dissertation.)*
- **Under-communication.** Kotter's list of eight errors in leading change (HBR 1995 article; book *Leading Change*, 1996) includes undercommunicating the vision "by a factor of 10 (or 100 or even 1,000)". This is a rule-of-thumb claim by an experienced observer, not a measured statistic; two summaries and a 1996 book review quote it the same way. The book itself was not read.
- **Forgetting.** Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve shows fast early loss. Murre & Dros (*PLOS ONE* 10(7), 2015) replicated it with one subject over about 70 hours of learning, relearning after 20 minutes to 31 days, and found a similar curve with a possible bump at 24 hours. In the replication, the 31-day savings were about 4%, below the 20% or so in earlier curves, so exact levels vary by study. *(Savings values for shorter gaps were not read.)*
- **Concrete messages.** Heath & Heath (2007) argue that concrete, specific messages are remembered better. *(Not yet verified; the book was not read.)*
- **The model.** The lab's model is illustrative: people forget exponentially between tellings, each telling rebuilds memory, each repeat lasts longer, and the effect of "tied to decisions" is assumed. It is tuned so one deck told once ends in single digits (2.4%), the same order as Newton's 2.5%, though that was a different task. Do not read its percentages as findings.

## Where it breaks
- **Small, close teams.** At 3-5 people who talk all day, repetition happens by itself and context is shared; the gap is much smaller.
- **Slogans without content.** A sentence people can repeat but not apply is worse than a clear paragraph; the example and the live case give it meaning.
- **Reference material still matters.** Investors, grant reviewers and new hires need the long version. The point is order: the sentence first, the detail on demand.
- **Repeating a wrong or changing strategy.** More repetition locks in whatever you say; if the strategy is unsettled, fix it before broadcasting it.
- **Culture and trust.** If people do not believe the strategy or if incentives contradict it, perfect recall will not produce aligned decisions.
- **The model is an average.** Real teams vary by person and role; the simulation shows shape, not a forecast.

## Application question (graded on rubric B)
> It is early 2027. Your new company has 12 engineers, 3 sales people and two pilot customers waiting. You have just spent a month deciding the strategy for the year and written an 8-page plan. The team nodded when you presented it.
> 1. Why is your sense that "they got it" probably too high? Name the law and the mechanism in your own words.
> 2. Roughly how large might the gap be after a month, and which people is it widest for?
> 3. What do you do in the next four weeks (format, frequency, channels, a way to test it)? Give a concrete policy.
> 4. Where could this approach mislead you in your company?

Answer in under 300 words.

## Takeaways
1. You overestimate how clearly your strategy lands: knowing the context makes it hard to imagine lacking it. After one detailed telling, a team may keep single digits while you guess three quarters.
2. Concrete and repeated beats complete: one sentence with a real example, repeated weekly in several channels, can work about 24 times better than one detailed deck.
3. The further a listener is from your background (sales, new hires), the larger the gap. Check the furthest group first, and never trust a silent room.
4. Tie the strategy to real decisions: live cases repeat it, train its use and show you the truth. A long document adds almost nothing.
