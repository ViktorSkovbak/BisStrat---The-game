# D01 reveal: what Intel did

## What happened
- **The "new CEO" test.** In *Only the Paranoid Survive* (1996), Grove tells how, in 1985, he asked Moore what a new CEO brought in by the board would do. Moore answered at once: get out of memories. Grove's reply, in substance: then why don't we walk out the door, come back in and do it ourselves? *(Paraphrased; the exact wording is in the book.)*
- **The exit.** Intel announced its withdrawal from DRAM in October 1985. Its own letter put DRAM at under 5% of the business and said 256K parts were selling below $3.
- **The cost.** Revenue fell about 16% in 1985 and profit essentially disappeared (EPS $0.01). In 1986 Intel lost about $173 million. About 7,200 jobs were cut over two years and eight older plants were closed *(job and plant figures from a reproduced 1986 shareholder letter; not yet checked against the original)*.
- **The 386.** Launched in October 1985 and, unusually, **sole-sourced**: Intel did not license a second source. Compaq shipped the first 386 PC in 1986, ahead of IBM.
- **The result.** In 1992 Intel became the world's largest semiconductor maker, with revenue of about $5.8 billion.
- **The hidden story (Burgelman, ASQ 1994).** The exit was ratified, not invented, at the top. Intel's internal resource-allocation rule (capacity to the highest margin per wafer) had already been moving manufacturing away from memory for years. Strategy was being made in the fabs before it was made in the boardroom.

## Why it worked (the mechanisms)
1. **Strategic inflection point (Grove):** a "10x" change in one competitive force (here, Japanese cost and capital) makes the old strategy fail no matter how well it is executed. Executing harder is the trap.
2. **Sunk-cost and identity:** the "technology driver" and "full line" arguments were partly true and partly identity defence. The fix was to keep the technology learning in other products (EPROMs, then microprocessors) rather than in a losing business.
3. **Resource allocation is the real strategy:** whatever the plan says, the strategy is what gets the capacity, money and best people (Burgelman; Rumelt's "coherent action").
4. **Capture the value of a winning position:** sole-sourcing the 386 turned a design win into pricing power. It was a bet that the PC clone market needed Intel more than Intel needed IBM's comfort.

## What a strong answer contained
- **~1500:** exit DRAM; the crux is a cost disadvantage that won't close; focus on microprocessors; accept layoffs.
- **~1800, adds:** names the identity and "technology driver" arguments and answers them (move process learning to EPROM and microprocessors); sequences the exit (stop new DRAM investment now, announce, redeploy fab capacity, keep key process engineers); treats the 386 second-source question as the big value decision and argues for sole-sourcing or a tightly limited licence; sets kill criteria on 386 demand.
- **2000+, adds:** sees that middle management has already exited and uses it (ratify, don't fight); quantifies the margin-per-wafer gap; anticipates the IBM relationship risk and the clone makers as the counterweight; thinks about what Intel becomes (a design-plus-manufacturing microprocessor company) and what that implies for the next ten years of capital spending.

## The lesson for your company
Ask the "new CEO" question monthly: if the board replaced you tomorrow, what would the new person stop doing at once? For a founder, the "memory business" is often the first product idea or the first market you fell in love with.
