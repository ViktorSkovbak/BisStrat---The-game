# D01. Intel, 1985: the memory business

**Your seat:** Andy Grove, President of Intel, in mid-1985. Gordon Moore is CEO and chairman. You run the company day to day.

**Hindsight warning:** this is a famous case. If you know what happened, argue as if you did not. The grade is on your reasoning, the facts you use and how you sequence your moves, not on matching history.

## The facts as of mid-1985
**The company**
- Intel was founded in 1968 as a memory company. Its 1103 (1970) was the first commercially successful DRAM. Memory is the company's identity: many senior managers still think of Intel as a memory company.
- 1984 was a record year: revenue of about $1.6 billion, after strong growth in 1983 and 1984.
- 1985 is collapsing: the whole chip industry is in a deep downturn, and Intel's revenue is falling sharply while profit is close to zero.

**Memory (DRAM)**
- Japanese makers (NEC, Hitachi, Fujitsu and others) passed US makers in 64K DRAM share around 1981 and have kept gaining. They are reported to run yields well above US makers' and to price aggressively.
- Intel's share of the world DRAM market has fallen to the low single digits (sources range from about 1% to 3%).
- 256K DRAM prices are falling steeply; the market is a commodity, and the Japanese makers have deep-pocketed parent groups that tolerate losses.
- DRAM is now a small part of Intel's sales (under about 5%) and is losing money.
- Arguments you hear inside the company: "DRAM is our technology driver: it is the high-volume product that keeps our process technology sharp." And from sales: "Customers want a full product line; without memory we lose the account."

**Microprocessors**
- IBM chose Intel's 8088 for the IBM PC (1981). The PC and its clones are spreading fast.
- IBM paid $250 million for a 12% stake in Intel at the end of 1982, and has since added to it on the open market.
- The 80386, a 32-bit microprocessor, is close to launch (autumn 1985). Industry practice is to license a second source so that big customers feel safe; IBM has expected this in the past.
- Your fab planners report that memory gets fewer wafer starts each quarter: the capacity allocation rule favours products with the highest margin per wafer, and microprocessors win. In practice, middle management has already been moving capacity away from DRAM.

**Other**
- Intel also makes EPROMs (another memory type), where it is still competitive.
- The downturn means any decision will come with layoffs and plant closures regardless.

## Your task (graded on the decision rubric)
Write under 450 words:
1. **Diagnosis:** what is really going on? Name the crux.
2. **Options:** at least three real alternatives.
3. **Decision:** what do you do with DRAM, with a stated confidence (%)?
4. **The next 12 months:** your first three moves, in order. Include what you do about the 386 and second-sourcing, and how you handle the people and identity side.
5. **Kill criteria:** what would make you reverse or change course, and by when?
